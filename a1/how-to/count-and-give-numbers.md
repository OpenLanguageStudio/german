# Count and give numbers

German numbers up to a hundred come from about twenty words and one pattern. Ages, phone numbers, prices, bus numbers and years all use them.

From thirteen on, German puts the smaller part first. *Dreizehn* is "three-ten", as English *thirteen* is. Above twenty, English changes the order and German does not: *dreiundzwanzig* is "three-and-twenty", where English says *twenty-three*. To count in German past twenty, then, the unit comes first and the ten comes last.

## When to use it

- Giving a mobile number to a doctor's receptionist on the phone
- Telling a waiter how many people are coming
- Checking the number of a bus at a stop
- Saying how long someone has lived in a city

## Key phrases

### Zero to twelve

Each of the numbers from 0 to 12 is its own word.

:::{ol-examples}
:presenters: lukas
null, eins, zwei, drei
: zero, one, two, three

vier, fünf, sechs
: four, five, six

sieben, acht, neun
: seven, eight, nine

zehn, elf, zwölf
: ten, eleven, twelve
:::

### Thirteen to nineteen

The teens add *zehn* to the unit, as English adds *-teen*. Two of them lose a few letters.

| Phrase | Notes |
|---|---|
| {stack}`dreizehn \| 13` | *drei* + *zehn*; 14, 15, 18 and 19 work the same way |
| {stack}`sechzehn \| 16` | the *s* of *sechs* drops |
| {stack}`siebzehn \| 17` | the *-en* of *sieben* drops |

### The tens

Most tens end in *-zig*.

| Phrase | Notes |
|---|---|
| {stack}`zwanzig \| 20` | not built from *zwei* |
| {stack}`dreißig \| 30` | the only ten that ends in *-ßig* |
| {stack}`sechzig, siebzig \| 60, 70` | the same short forms as 16 and 17 |
| {stack}`hundert \| 100` | also *einhundert*; both are common |

### From twenty-one

Between the tens, the unit comes first, then *und*, then the ten, all written as one word. *Eins* loses its *s*: *einundzwanzig*.

:::{ol-examples}
:presenters: lukas
einundzwanzig, zweiundzwanzig
: twenty-one, twenty-two

dreiunddreißig
: thirty-three

siebenundvierzig
: forty-seven
:::

## Counting to a hundred and beyond

Above a hundred, the same parts join up: *hundertzwanzig* (120), *zweihundert* (200), *tausend* (1,000). A German speaker writes 1.000 with a point and 12,50 with a comma.

## Years and phone numbers

A year before 2000 is said in hundreds, as in English: 1993 is *neunzehnhundertdreiundneunzig*. From 2000 on, it is a thousand plus the rest: 2015 is *zweitausendfünfzehn*. Phone numbers are read digit by digit or in pairs. On the phone, *zwo* often replaces *zwei*, so that it does not sound like *drei*. More on giving a number is in [Give your personal details](give-your-personal-details.md).

## Summary

:::{admonition} Key points
:class: note

- 0 to 12 are single words. The teens add *zehn*: *dreizehn*, *vierzehn*.
- From 21, the unit comes first: *einundzwanzig*, *neunundneunzig*.
- *Sechzehn*, *siebzehn*, *sechzig* and *siebzig* drop letters, and *dreißig* ends in *-ßig*.
- Years before 2000 go in hundreds, later years in thousands. *Zwo* stands for *zwei* on the phone.
:::

:::{admonition} Common mistakes
:class: caution

- *\*zwanzigeins* → **einundzwanzig** (the unit comes first, joined by *und*)
- *\*einsundzwanzig* → **einundzwanzig** (*eins* loses its *s* inside a number)
- *\*sechszehn*, *\*siebenzehn* → **sechzehn**, **siebzehn**
- *\*in 2015* → **2015** or **im Jahr 2015** (a bare year takes no *in*)
:::
