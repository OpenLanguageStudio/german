# Say hello and goodbye

German greetings open almost every exchange, at a bakery counter as much as among friends. Hello and goodbye in German follow two things: the time of day, and whether the people say *Sie* or *du*.

*Guten Tag* and *Auf Wiedersehen* suit offices, doctors and first meetings, where people say *Sie*. *Hallo* and *Tschüss* suit friends and family. In shops and cafés they are heard with *Sie* too. [Choose between du and Sie](choose-between-du-and-sie.md) covers that choice in full.

## When to use it

- Walking into a bakery and greeting the staff
- Arriving at the reception desk of a doctor's practice
- Meeting a neighbour on the stairs
- Leaving a friend's flat after dinner
- Ending a phone call with an office

## Key phrases

### Saying hello

| Phrase | Notes |
|---|---|
| {stack}`Guten Morgen! \| Good morning!` | until about 10 or 11; among friends often just *Morgen!* |
| {stack}`Guten Tag! \| Hello!` | the neutral daytime greeting, literally "good day" |
| {stack}`Guten Abend! \| Good evening!` | from about 6 pm |
| {stack}`Hallo! \| Hello! / Hi!` | {bdg-light}`informal`, any time of day |

### Saying goodbye

| Phrase | Notes |
|---|---|
| {stack}`Auf Wiedersehen! \| Goodbye!` | {bdg-light}`formal`, often shortened to *Wiedersehen!* |
| {stack}`Auf Wiederhören! \| Goodbye!` | {bdg-light}`formal`, on the phone only: "until we hear each other again" |
| {stack}`Tschüss! \| Bye!` | the everyday goodbye, with *du* and often with *Sie* |
| {stack}`Bis bald! \| See you soon!` | *bis* with a time: *bis morgen*, *bis später* |
| {stack}`Gute Nacht! \| Good night!` | when parting late at night or going to bed, never as a greeting |

### With Sie and with du

The greeting, the question *Wie geht's?* (how are you?) and the goodbye all change with the register.

## Greetings with Sie

With *Sie*, a greeting often adds *Herr* or *Frau* and the surname. The reply repeats the greeting. A wish such as *Schönen Tag noch!* (have a nice day) gets *Danke, gleichfalls!* (thanks, same to you).

:::{culture-note}
In a doctor's waiting room or a small lift, people often greet the whole room with *Guten Tag* and say *Wiedersehen* on the way out. In small shops, a hello and a goodbye to the staff are part of every visit.
:::

## Greetings with du

Between friends, greetings get shorter. *Morgen!* stands for *Guten Morgen*, and *Na?* on its own means "hi, how's things?". *Wie geht's?* is short for *Wie geht es dir?*

:::{culture-note}
*Moin* is the everyday greeting in the north of Germany, at any time of day. In the south and in Austria, people say *Grüß Gott* and *Servus*. In Berlin, *Hallo* and *Tschüss* cover most of the day.
:::

## Summary

:::{admonition} Key points
:class: note

- *Guten Morgen*, *Guten Tag* and *Guten Abend* follow the clock. *Hallo* works at any time.
- *Auf Wiedersehen* is the formal goodbye, *Auf Wiederhören* its phone version. *Tschüss* is the everyday one.
- *Gute Nacht* is only for parting late at night or for bedtime.
- *Wie geht es Ihnen?* goes with *Sie*, *Wie geht's?* with *du*.
:::

:::{admonition} Common mistakes
:class: caution

- *\*Gute Nacht!* when leaving a shop at 7 pm → **Schönen Abend noch!** (*Gute Nacht* belongs to bedtime)
- *\*Wie geht es Sie?* → Wie geht es **Ihnen**? (the question takes the dative form; learn more in [Personal pronouns: dative](/a1/grammar/pronouns/personalpronomen-dativ.md))
- *\*Ich bin gut.* as the answer to *Wie geht's?* → **Gut, danke.** or **Mir geht's gut.** (*Ich bin gut* means "I'm good at it"; learn more in [Say how you feel](say-how-you-feel.md))
:::
